Section 01 — What Is a Prompt?
01 / UNDERSTANDING PROMPTS

Your Words Are the Paintbrush

An AI image generator does not think visually — it thinks linguistically. When you describe an image in text, the model interprets your words, cross-references patterns learned from millions of images, and constructs a visual output. The text you write to guide this process is called a prompt.

Think of a prompt as giving detailed directions to a highly capable but entirely literal assistant. The more precise and structured your instructions, the closer the result will match your mental image. A vague prompt produces a vague image; a focused prompt produces a focused one.

1girl warrior princess , ancient battlefield , sunset , watercolor medium , best quality , very aesthetic

Every prompt is really a combination of several layers: what exists in the scene (subjects and objects), where it is (setting and background), how it looks (style and medium), and how good it should be (quality tags). Keeping these layers in mind as you write will dramatically improve your results from the start.

💡
The AI reads your prompt from left to right, and the elements you mention first carry the most weight. Always open with what matters most — usually your subject.
Section 02 — Prose vs. Tags
02 / TWO APPROACHES

Prose vs. Tags — Choosing Your Language

You can communicate with an image model using two distinct styles of writing. Both are valid, but they produce noticeably different results — and understanding the difference is one of the first real skills to develop as a prompter.

✦ Tag-Based (Recommended)

1girl, long silver hair, green eyes, enchanted forest, moonlight, best quality, very aesthetic

Short, comma-separated descriptors. Each tag is a direct, unambiguous instruction the model handles efficiently. The AI is trained on data structured this way, so it interprets tags with high precision.

✕ Prose-Based (Less Efficient)

"A young girl with long silver hair and green eyes is standing in an enchanted forest bathed in beautiful moonlight."

Natural sentences are intuitive, but filler words like "is," "and," "in," and "a" consume token space without adding visual meaning. The model also parses sentence structure less reliably than tags.

The rule of thumb is straightforward: if a word does not describe something you can see in the image, it is probably taking up space without contributing. Nouns, adjectives, and specific visual descriptors are your most powerful tools.

"As you type, the AI may offer tag suggestions. The more opaque or bright the suggestion indicator, the better the model understands that tag — prioritize those for more predictable results."
Section 03 — Token Limits & Prompt Order
03 / STRUCTURE & LIMITS

How the AI Reads Your Prompt

Every AI model has a maximum number of tokens it can process per prompt. A token is roughly one word or word-fragment. Exceeding this limit causes the model to silently discard the overflow — meaning the tags you placed at the very end may simply be ignored.

Most interfaces display a progress bar as you type, showing how much of your token budget remains. Keep an eye on it. More importantly, make every word earn its place: long or redundant prompts do not improve quality, they just waste budget.

Token Budget Strategy

A practical way to think about your token budget is to divide your prompt into tiers by importance, then write accordingly:

  • Subject first. State who or what the image is about — character type, count, defining features.

  • Setting second. Where the scene takes place — environment, background, lighting, time of day.

  • Style third. Artistic medium, era, rendering style, camera angle.

  • Quality last. Broad quality enhancers like best quality or very aesthetic go at the end — they are effective even when placed there.

Prompt Order — Why Position Matters

The AI does not treat all parts of your prompt equally. Elements placed earlier exert a stronger influence on the final image. This is especially true in anime-style models, where tag order is a documented, deliberate feature of the training.

Section 04 — Emphasis Control
04 / EMPHASIS CONTROL

Strengthening & Weakening Tags

Beyond position, you have a powerful second lever for controlling how much attention the AI pays to any given element: weight vectors. Using curly braces {} and square brackets [], you can amplify or suppress specific parts of your prompt.

Syntax Relative Influence on Output Multiplier
[[[tag]]]
× 0.86
[tag]
× 0.95
tag
× 1.00
{tag}
× 1.05
{{tag}}
× 1.10
{{{tag}}}
× 1.16

Each additional pair of braces multiplies or divides the element's weight by approximately 1.05. These effects are cumulative, so nesting three pairs delivers roughly a 16% boost. The math is less important than developing an intuition for when to use it.

Example // Without emphasis — chibi style appears, but may be subtle 1girl, black hair, chibi, catgirl under a cherry blossom tree // Strongly emphasized — chibi is the dominant visual style 1girl, black hair, {{{chibi}}}, catgirl under a cherry blossom tree // Deliberately suppressed — chibi barely registers 1girl, black hair, [[[chibi]]], catgirl under a cherry blossom tree
Important Rule
The Undesired Content Field Reverses This Logic

Every image generator provides an "Undesired Content" or "Negative Prompt" field — a place to list elements you want the AI to avoid. When you place braces inside this field, the behavior flips: {watermark} means "avoid this very strongly," while [text] means "avoid this only slightly." Always account for this reversal.

Section 05 — Your First Prompt
05 / PUTTING IT TOGETHER

Building Your First Structured Prompt

With the four concepts above in mind — layer structure, tag-based writing, prompt order, and emphasis control — you are ready to construct a real prompt from scratch. The following walkthrough builds one layer at a time, showing how each addition shapes the final output.

Goal — A Fantasy Background Scene

  • Start with the subject. Since we want no characters, we use the explicit tag:
    no humans

  • Define the setting. Add the main environment and atmospheric condition:
    no humans, flower field, sunset

  • Specify the artistic style. Apply a medium tag to shape the visual feel:
    no humans, flower field, sunset, watercolor medium

  • Add quality and aesthetic tags. Place these last to polish the result:
    no humans, flower field, sunset, watercolor medium, best quality, very aesthetic

  • Optionally, emphasize the focal element. Wrap your most important setting tag:
    no humans, {{{flower field}}}, sunset, watercolor medium, best quality, very aesthetic

Final Prompt no humans, {{{flower field}}}, sunset, watercolor medium, best quality, very aesthetic

You now have a prompt that respects all four foundational principles: it uses tags instead of prose, follows a logical layer order, stays comfortably within token limits, and applies emphasis where it will be most effective. That is a stronger foundation than most beginners start with.

"The key to improving your prompts is iteration. Generate, observe where the output diverges from your intention, adjust one thing at a time, and regenerate. Prompting is a feedback loop — not a one-shot process."
Part I — Summary
Core Concepts to Remember

  • Prompts are layered. Subject → Setting → Style → Quality is the recommended order.

  • Tags outperform prose. Concise descriptors are processed more reliably than full sentences.

  • Position determines priority. Earlier tags carry more influence on the final image.

  • { } and [ ] give you fine control. Nest them carefully to amplify or suppress any element.

  • Iterate deliberately. Change one variable at a time to understand what each tag contributes.
