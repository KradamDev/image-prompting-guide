Section 01 — Mixing Styles & Mediums
01 / STYLE FUSION

Mixing Styles & Mediums

One of the most creatively rewarding capabilities of AI image generation is its ability to combine artistic styles and visual mediums that would be difficult or impossible to achieve through traditional means. A single prompt can instruct the model to render an image simultaneously in the manner of two distinct traditions — blending, for instance, the warm textures of watercolour with the high-contrast neon palette of cyberpunk illustration.

Style tags and medium tags are distinct in their function. A medium tag describes the physical or digital material through which the image appears to have been produced — ink, watercolour, graphite, 3D render. A style tag describes the aesthetic or genre of the work — cyberpunk, renaissance, impressionist, fantasy. Combining one medium with one style is the most legible approach, particularly for beginners, as both dimensions remain clearly interpretable by the model.

Style + Medium Combinations
Medium Tag Style Tag Expected Character
watercolor medium fantasy landscape Soft, luminous environments with organic colour blending and visible brushwork
ink sketch medium cyberpunk style High-contrast line art with neon colour fills; urban, gritty compositional energy
3D render medium sci-fi style Photorealistic surface materials with futuristic design language and metallic sheen
graphite sketch medium renaissance style Classical compositional gravity rendered in monochrome pencil with dramatic shading

Era tags — such as 1980s style, Renaissance, or year 2014 — operate as a specialised subset of style tags. They instruct the model to adopt the visual conventions of a specific historical period, drawing on its training data to approximate the colour grading, compositional norms, and artistic idioms of that era. Results vary depending on how well the target period is represented in the model's training set, but for widely documented periods they can be remarkably effective.

Style Fusion Examples // Painterly science-fiction futuristic city, watercolor medium, sci-fi style, neon lighting, best quality // Retro illustration with ink texture forest temple, ink sketch medium, 1970s style, muted tones, very aesthetic // Classical character in modern rendering 1girl, royal throne room, 3D render medium, renaissance style, dramatic lighting
💡
When fusing two styles that feel conceptually distant — such as a classical medium with a modern genre — use emphasis brackets to clarify which dimension should dominate. Writing {{{watercolor medium}}} alongside cyberpunk style makes clear that the medium, not the genre, should set the primary visual tone.
Section 02 — Building a Consistent Character
02 / CHARACTER CREATION

Constructing a Consistent Character

Creating a character who appears recognisably consistent across multiple generated images is one of the most challenging and rewarding skills in AI prompting. The model has no persistent memory of characters between sessions — every generation begins from scratch. The only mechanism for consistency is the prompt itself. A character whose visual properties are described with sufficient precision and tagged in the correct order will appear reliably similar across many generations, provided the seed and core tags are preserved.

Character construction is built from five distinct layers, each addressed by its own category of tags. Understanding each layer — and the specific tags available within it — gives you the vocabulary to describe a character with the depth and specificity required for cross-generation consistency.

Begin every character prompt with the subject tag (1girl, 1boy) followed immediately by solo if only one character is intended. This prevents the model from spontaneously adding background figures — a common occurrence when the subject position is left ambiguous.

Step-by-Step: A Travelling Sorceress

The following sequence demonstrates how to build a consistent character incrementally, adding one tag layer at a time. This approach makes it straightforward to identify which addition changes the output and which leaves it unchanged — which is essential knowledge when refining a character across multiple sessions.

  • Establish the subject and framing.
    1girl, solo, full body

  • Define hair — length, style, and colour.
    + swept bangs, bob cut, medium hair, platinum blonde hair

  • Add eyes and skin.
    + aqua eyes, pale skin

  • Introduce broad clothing tags.
    + witch hat, blue robe, skinny, slim legs

  • Refine clothing with specific items.
    + corset, leather belt, leather pouch, brown gloves, knee boots

  • Detail the lower outfit and add quality tags.
    + short skirt, blue skirt, frilled skirt, black pantyhose, best quality

Final Character Prompt 1girl, solo, full body, swept bangs, bob cut, medium hair, platinum blonde hair, aqua eyes, pale skin, witch hat, blue headwear, blue cape, white shirt, long sleeves, corset, leather belt, leather pouch, short skirt, blue skirt, frilled skirt, black pantyhose, brown gloves, knee boots, skinny, slim legs, best quality
"The balance between too many tags and too few is a genuine craft. An over-specified prompt can cause the model to generate visual noise rather than clarity — too many clothing items competing for space, for instance, often produces incoherent layering. Aim for sufficient specificity, not exhaustive completeness."
⚠️
Tags that describe front-facing features — such as eye colour, freckles, or abdominal detail — can override a camera angle instruction like from behind, pulling the character forward to display those features. If a back-facing pose is essential, keep front-facing feature tags minimal or remove them entirely.
Section 03 — Framing & Camera Angles
03 / FRAMING & CAMERA

Framing Tags & Camera Angles

Framing and camera angle tags govern the compositional relationship between the viewer and the subject — how close the camera appears, at what elevation it sits, and from which direction it looks. These tags do not modify the character's appearance; they modify the image's perspective on the character. Selecting the appropriate framing tag is, in effect, making a directorial decision about how your scene will be experienced.

"The canvas aspect ratio and the framing tag should work together, not against each other. A full body tag on a square canvas often produces an awkwardly cropped figure. Pair full-body framing with a vertical 2:3 canvas, and landscape or wide-shot framing with a horizontal 16:9 canvas, for naturally proportioned compositions."
Section 04 — Multi-Character Prompts
04 / MULTIPLE CHARACTERS

Structuring Multi-Character Prompts

When a scene involves more than one character, the prompt structure must be deliberately engineered to prevent the model from applying one character's attributes to another. Without a clear structural framework, the AI tends to blend traits unpredictably — giving the wrong hair colour to the wrong figure, or merging their clothing into a confused hybrid. The solution is identifier-based prompting: assigning a unique letter or label to each character and consistently using that label as the prefix for all attributes belonging to that figure.

Identifier Structure Colour-coded by character
Count + Identifier Declaration
2girls_A_and_B
Character A — Attributes
{A girl have (white hair) (blue eyes) (white frill dress) (white bow)}
Character B — Attributes
{B girl have (black hair) (red eyes) (black frill dress) (black bow)}
Shared Scene + Quality
cinematic lighting, lively smile, sitting on couch, best quality

Each character's attributes are enclosed in curly braces and prefixed with their identifier. Scene elements — setting, lighting, shared actions, and quality tags — are placed outside the identifier blocks, where they apply globally to the image rather than to a specific figure. This separation is the key structural principle of multi-character prompting.

As the number of characters increases, the prompt necessarily grows more complex, and the model's ability to honour each character's individual attributes decreases proportionally. Two and three-character scenes are reliably achievable. Beyond five characters, attribute blending becomes increasingly unpredictable, and the canvas resolution must be widened substantially to give the model enough compositional space to distinguish between figures.

Interaction Prompts — Couples // Hugging 1girl, 1boy, guy: grey eyes, short hair, girl: black hair, red eyes, couple, hugging, natural lighting, outdoors, masterpiece // Princess carry 1girl, 1boy, guy: grey eyes, short hair, girl: very long hair, red eyes, couple, princess carry, white background, masterpiece // Holding hands, wide landscape 1girl, 1boy, couple, holding hand, natural lighting, outdoors, long shot, landscape, lively smile, masterpiece
Composition Styles for Groups
Tailoring the Visual Format to the Scene

Beyond individual character attributes, the overall compositional style of a group image can be directed with a few powerful tags. movie poster introduces a dramatic, cinematic arrangement with subjects positioned for visual impact. view from above creates an intimate bird's-eye perspective, suggesting closeness and warmth. view from below conveys scale and authority — the figures appear imposing from the viewer's lower vantage point. view from back frames the group as observers themselves, inviting the viewer into the scene rather than presenting the characters to them.

Increasing Character Count

Replace the numeric subject tag accordingly: 3girls, 5girls, 10 girls. Extend the identifier list to match the count — A through J for ten characters. Widen the canvas to a horizontal resolution as the count grows, to give each figure sufficient compositional room.

Undesired Content for Groups

When generating large groups, the Undesired Content field becomes especially important. Adding extra limbs, bad anatomy, missing fingers, and blurry as negative prompts helps the model maintain anatomical coherence when managing multiple figures simultaneously.

Section 05 — Comic Panel Prompting
05 / COMIC PANELS

Sequential Storytelling with Panel Prompts

One of the more sophisticated applications of structured prompting is directing the AI to generate images that follow a comic panel layout — sequential frames arranged within a single image, each depicting a distinct moment in a narrative. This technique extends the identifier-based approach used in multi-character prompting, applying it now to panels rather than figures: each panel receives its own label, and the scene within that panel is described inside its own structural block.

The foundational principle is straightforward: declare the total number of panels and assign each an identifier at the beginning of the prompt, then describe the content of each panel individually. Shared attributes — such as recurring characters and their visual properties — are defined once and referenced consistently across panels. This consistency of reference is what gives the AI enough information to maintain character continuity from one frame to the next.

Panel Prompt Anatomy
Three Structural Components

  • Panel count and identifier declaration. Open the prompt by specifying the format and naming each panel. Format: NPanel-Comic_X_and_Y where N is the number of panels and X, Y are the panel labels.

  • Character role assignments. Define each recurring character and their narrative role once, so the model can apply those definitions consistently across all panels. Format: {"{A-Boy is Samurai}"}

  • Individual panel descriptions. Describe the content, action, and setting of each panel inside its own braced block. Format: {"{X-Panel: scene description}"}. Use double braces {{"{{"}} to emphasise dramatic elements.

HEADER — Count + Identifiers
3Panel-Comic_X_and_Y_and_Z
PANEL X
{X-Panel: 1girl,
{{Girl wears explorer's gear,
standing by cliff edge,
looking out}}}
PANEL Y
{Y-Panel: 1girl_and_1boy,
{Girl and Boy hiking,
mountain in distance}}
PANEL Z
{Z-Panel: 1girl_and_1boy,
{Girl and Boy setting up tent,

For action sequences, the progression of verbs across panels carries the narrative momentum. Using distinct, active language within each panel description — such as {A-Boy charging forward} transitioning to {A-Boy swinging sword} — gives the model enough directional information to generate a visually coherent sequence rather than three unrelated images placed in a grid.

Two-Panel Action Sequence 2Panel-Comic_A_and_B, // Panel A — Preparation {A-Panel: 1boy_A, {{A-Boy is Samurai, holding sword, stance-ready}}, {background: training dojo}}, // Panel B — Execution {B-Panel: 1boy_A, {A-Boy sweating, concentration face, practicing with straw dummy}}
🎬
Maintain character identifiers consistently across all panels. If a character is introduced as A-Boy in the first panel, every subsequent reference to that same figure should use A-Boy — not "the boy" or "him." Inconsistent naming is the most common cause of character continuity breakdown in multi-panel prompts.
Part III — Summary
From Single Characters to Sequential Narratives

Style fusion enables creative combinations of artistic mediums and genre aesthetics — use medium tags and style tags together, with emphasis brackets to clarify which should dominate. Character creation requires a layered tag vocabulary covering subject, hair, eyes, skin, body type, and clothing — applied in the correct order for consistent results across generations. Framing and camera angle tags make directorial decisions explicit; pair them deliberately with canvas dimensions for maximum compositional control. Multi-character prompts use identifier-based structuring to prevent attribute blending between figures, with shared scene tags placed outside the identifier blocks. Comic panel prompts extend this structure into sequential narratives, with consistent character naming and progressive action verbs providing continuity across frames.

Each of these techniques is, at its core, a more disciplined application of the foundational principles from Parts I and II: precise tag selection, logical ordering, and deliberate emphasis. The tools are the same; the compositions are simply more ambitious.
